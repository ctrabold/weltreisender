## Saturday, 23 January 2010

Been Through the Mill

**The first kayak day** starts at seven in the morning. In a flash the bags are on the bike, so I can devote myself to the breakfast buffet in peace. A breakfast buffet - a luxury I haven't had for months (Delingha, China) (and haven't missed much either).

No question: the energy reserves have to be full to the brim for the adventure ahead!

Three days of murderous rapids, exhausting manoeuvres round rocks and metre-long (and therefore potentially dangerous) Irrawaddy freshwater dolphins. We might even have to make an emergency landing on one of the 4,000 Islands because we run out of puff or the boat springs a leak. All that in this sweltering heat!

With these thoughts in mind I roll up at the travel agency just before eight, park Rondolf in a safe corner and wait, stuffed and excited, for departure.

There are three other people on the trip in the minibus. A couple from South Tyrol, Italy, and a man from Heidelberg, Germany.

A few minutes later the man from Heidelberg finally breaks the early-morning silence, and all four of us fall into cheerful small talk about travelling, in English. Main topic - as so often - China!

With more than three months there and some juicy stories from the road, I can happily spill a fair few beans.

At some point one of us lets slip some German word or other, and amid roars of laughter we realise we can all speak German. That happens a lot and is always funny.

The storytelling and the bus both gallop on over hill and dale, and far too soon we reach the harbour. The man from Heidelberg travels on; Manu, Tobi and I walk on to the beach.

Only moments ago lord of all the cycle paths of the southern hemisphere, I shrink to the size of a jerboa at the sight of the boats, life jackets and bright yellow crash helmets.

But it's not as bad as all that: the life jacket fits, and after a short safety and paddling briefing we're already wobbling across the calm water. No helmets for now.

Until lunch my paddle strokes are still a bit unsteady. After that it goes better and better and is great fun.

Manu and Tobi paddle their own double kayak and turn out to be very likeable companions. My initial fear has vanished, and we glide calmly through breathtaking river scenery to the 4,000 Islands.

In the end we reach our destination on Don Khon, a very quiet and fairly unspoilt island, soaking wet but relaxed and pleasantly tired.

There, after a good dinner and a short briefing for tomorrow's tour, it's off to the bamboo hut, where I very quickly fall very deeply asleep.

**The second day** begins with a journey back in time to the days of French colonial rule. Then we visit the historic sites on foot and, after the big waterfall, continue our kayak trip from a quiet bay to Dolphin Bay.

For the first time it's "helmets on", because the first rapids are waiting for us.

We end the evening on a tiny rocky island, keeping an eagle eye out for the freshwater dolphins.

A few times we see the mammals' dorsal fins, then it's time to say goodbye. Tobi and Manu have only booked two days and are now heading back to the guesthouse. I head out again with Tae, my guide - closer to the dolphins.

Beautiful but shy, these creatures. Sadly, you often only hear them snort as they come up for air, and then they've dived again. A real encounter (like the one I once had diving in the Red Sea) is not to be had here.

No wonder, with all the loudly rattling motorboats! The moment you catch even a hint of a dolphin, the boats laden with pasty tourists roar off in the new direction... If I were a dolphin, I wouldn't feel like doing much more than occasionally sticking my backside out of the water either. _Eat this!_

Before dinner I cool off once more in the clear, soft Mekong water and swim a lap to a little island in the river.

Unfortunately dinner at the "homestay" isn't so good, and the half-cooked fish really upsets my stomach. On top of that, a villager blasts the whole village all night with Laotian funeral music on an electric organ.

The guide explains the Laotian mourning custom to me: before cremation, the deceased lies in state at home for three days. That way every friend of the family and every villager can say a dignified farewell. The more people, the better. The music is meant to draw attention to the death and at the same time invite people to come by.

After we've visited the family I can sleep a bit better. Still, the feeling is similar to the one back in the Czech Republic (see Mourning for a Stranger), and the images and the meditatively repeating melody will stay with me for a long time.

**On day three** I wake up crumpled and mentally pretty shredded. I'm not in the mood for any shenanigans on the water today, and with my head hanging I cancel the canoe trip through the rapids to the big waterfall.

The cramps in my stomach that follow show me, though, that I'm absolutely right. A shame, but in this state there's no point.

All I want is to get to my bike as quickly as possible - or rather to the first-aid kit there, with which I can get myself ready for the onward journey to Phnom Penh in Cambodia.

Because my top priority is to get to as big a city as possible, so that I can see a competent doctor if my condition gets worse.

And so, just before 12 o'clock, I'm sitting on the bus to the Cambodian capital, leaving Laos slightly rushed and completely drenched in sweat. The country that, with its relaxed atmosphere, brought (back) so much joy of travelling for me.

On the Cambodian side the bus is waiting, and Rondolf is unceremoniously stuffed into it and buried under rucksacks weighing a ton. Brave Rondolf buried under a mountain of backpacker ballast - the indignity!

Planned arrival is 7:30 pm. I grab the last seat and simply hope that everything goes well until then and that everything stays watertight.
