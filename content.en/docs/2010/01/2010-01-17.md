## Sunday, 17 January 2010

Chaotic Finger Exercises

​16 January

Early in the morning I ask the guesthouse owner about getting on to Luang Prabang.

In doing so I stick strictly to a lesson I've learnt: when you're looking for real insider tips - ask the locals! Before you throw yourself into the fray on the road with lots of other travellers, a little chat with the "locals" can often tell you more in less time (doesn't apply to asking the way in the countryside).

Especially as the conversation is refreshingly multilingual: he - a teacher by trade - speaks French as well as English, and even coaxes one or two teeny-tiny Francophile crumbs out of my language centre (as everyone knows, I speak "good" French).

Meanwhile my head keeps drifting back into China small-talk mode (hahahau hau, eh eh!) and so on.

It always takes about two weeks before I acclimatise linguistically and can get into the new language and way of talking - or rather, before my hand-mouth coordination has switched over to the new country.

In the end I do find out how things stand with the boat trip to Luang Prabang. I also ask about taking a bike on the speedboats, which do the trip in 6 hours instead of 2 days.

Because I have clear requirements for the trip to LP: it should be fast (no two-day tour) and without a lot of Thai backpacker trappings, which got on my nerves within minutes of arriving in town.

His suggestion: we drive overland to Luang Prabang city in his jeep. That's 100% safe and muuuch cheaper. He can also tell me a lot about the country and its people on the way, AND I can take some nice pictures of the unspoilt interior.

I accept his suggestion and then enjoy a relaxing ride round the block, really letting my legs, soul and tongue hang loose.

In the evening we load the bike into the car and drive straight on: _We go Party!_ And there I was, happily tucking into a papaya!

Half an hour later I'm queuing in a long line of people with him and his wife: he's taking me to a Laotian wedding (Dutch giant marries Laotian woman half his height). The whole town seems to be gathering here to congratulate them.

We take our seats - of course - in the front row. And yes: everyone has to dance. Beads of sweat on my forehead, I mentally run through the basic steps I can call up. Nothing! Oh no! Stage fright, blackout - total disgrace!

Luckily I still have a bit of time, because even though the music is deafeningly loud, nobody seems to be paying any attention to the goings-on around the stage, let alone the entertainer; they're all just piling into the food.

Unthinkable back home: at the entrance the bride and groom are still receiving guests, while at the back people are already greedily ripping the cling film off the place settings (fly protection) and tucking in for all they're worth.

At some point the bride and groom finally sit down. Ten seconds later, the obligatory group photo, and straight after that the opening dance.

A traditional Laotian dance in which above all the hands are turned with fingers spread. Hip movements are barely visible. The same goes for turns and contact between partners. It strikes me as rather stiff and unemotional.

One by one, the parents and close relatives follow the couple until a closed circle forms.

Then the couple goes from table to table and every guest drinks a schnapps to their health. In between, another schnapps server scurries, crouching, through the crowd, pouring generously (a Laotian party starter?).

The upshot: after an hour I'm well and truly tipsy, because the scurrier apparently comes by my seat relatively often. Finally, the driver's wife asks me to dance, and there's no escape:

We go round in pairs in a circle, hands always turning, step by step around the big bouquet of flowers in front of the stage. Tentatively I wiggle my hips, trying to imagine something oriental and mystical.

When I close my eyes for a moment to give myself over completely to the music, my partner pinches me and we switch sides with a turn. Wow!

After the dance we leave the venue all at once along with hundreds of other guests and drive home. Now those are customs for you. It was certainly fun! Thanks to the (still unknown to me) host!

To be honest, I'm quite glad I don't have to drink any more. Being plied from all sides non-stop is more annoying than amusing in the end. Example: my strategic water is simply poured away without comment and topped up with warm beer...

Never mind: despite the VIP service and the shock absorbers, tomorrow will be very tiring and bumpy. I'm curious about this "new" way of travelling.
