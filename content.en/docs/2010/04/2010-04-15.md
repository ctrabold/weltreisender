## Thursday, 15 April 2010

Raub - Copy of a Chinese Nightmare

​15 April

If my Malaysian host is to be believed, the coming kilometres run along very good roads through glorious jungle.

Unfortunately, locals' assessments of how "bike-friendly" a road is tend to be rather questionable. But this time there really are plenty of good reasons to believe him:

Among those in the know, the route is famous for the annual "Le Tour de Langkawi" bike race ([http://www.ltdl.com.my/2010/therace/stage6.asp](http://www.ltdl.com.my/2010/therace/stage6.asp)), where international riders compete too. That gives me hope of a cycle-friendly environment.

Most of the traffic flows along the new highway in the valley. So the chances of some undisturbed moments in the hills of the "old road" are pretty good.

I'd already noticed the excellent road surface yesterday. Perfect!

And then there was that father and son on road bikes coming the other way. And at breakfast, two racing cyclists duly ride past us.

Yesterday's ordeal is forgotten, I'm really excited about today's ride and push off with a good feeling.

It's pleasantly mild, the forest is waking up: a good day is beginning!

The climb is steady but pleasant. I quickly find my rhythm and enjoy every metre. Only the gorgeous views force me to stop. Otherwise everything flows.

Even the traffic is barely noticeable. I can count the cars on one hand, and when one passes me there's no pointless honking - on the contrary, I get respectful smiles, approving applause or a brief encouraging chat through the side window.

Often, though, I simply stop and listen into the green.

All that knocking, chirping, whistling, humming and buzzing, set against the rush of the river and the trees, is truly breathtaking!

It takes some concentration to pick out the individual sounds from the dense carpet of noise and to withstand the audiovisual flood.

It's fascinating how the jumble suddenly sorts itself out and patterns, or individual animals, become recognisable. Combined with the fresh, clear air, these are very intense and above all very rare moments of deep connection with nature.

How much I've missed this! And how boring, monotonous and stupid the same old carpet of big-city noise sounds by comparison.

Sadly, the summit - and with it the farewell to the Genting Highlands - comes far too soon.

After that it's downhill all the way. The descent is lovely too and fairly quiet, but I can feel the traffic and noise coming back, until in Bentong they're completely dominant again.

There the road joins Highway Number 8, and everything on wheels heading for Kota Bharu converges on this road.

The landscape becomes irrelevant. It makes no difference what the surroundings look like, because from now on the road demands my full attention. There's no shade from trees or any other protection from the sun.

Instead, the black clouds of soot, the annoying rattle of broken exhausts and, above all, the needless honking are back!

No matter how nicely or encouragingly the honking is meant: a plain honk, as used here by 99% of drivers, is and always will be a warning signal to me, and is instinctively linked to danger and the corresponding physical reactions.

And maybe that's exactly what it is: a warning to finally put an end to this madness.

Sadly, it isn't that simple: my destination for the day is the town with the charming name of Raub (German for "robbery"). And the road there drags on.

As it turns out, the town lives up to its name: when I finally get there, I'm greeted by a utterly dreary, concrete-saturated town that robs me of my last hope of a dignified end to the day.

I've seen a lot, but the hotels here are almost unbeatable for ugliness: at the "Hotel Raub" I'm genuinely scared to go in. The mouldy concrete box with its gaping mouth just looks repulsive. What on earth might it look like inside?

Probably just like each of the three other "hotels" I look at afterwards.

They're all built the same way, Chinese-run and conveniently right next to each other: a massive concrete facade with rickety windows and a narrow entrance with an even narrower corridor up to the first floor. Waiting there is an old Chinese woman (for customers?).

The TV is blaring the usual Chinese soap-opera drivel at full volume. Communication - even with my Chinese dictionary - is very laborious.

The unfriendly lady immediately sends me two unmistakable and, it seems to me, quintessentially Chinese signals:

1. What the hell are you doing here? I hope you've got a good reason for disturbing me!
2. Only if I'm feeling gracious will I give you, you good-for-nothing, my last free room, and you'll pay me the price I name. No discussion. You've got no choice anyway.

Even downstairs I already know how dreadful the rooms are, and I'm always proved right.

And no matter how friendly and calm I stay: all my attempts at haggling are shot down. At the third attempt I grit my teeth and accept my fate.

It's grotty, it's too expensive, but all I want now is to sleep.

Luckily the shop next door has a bedtime treat for me: my favourite chocolate milk, which quickly soothes me and sends me off to sleep, full.
