## Tuesday, 20 April 2010

T-1000 Versus the Sun

​20 April
From Kota Bharu to Kota Besut

The ride from Kota Bharu to the port of Kota Besut is a trip from hell. No shade, just sun and, as always, lots of traffic.

Plus a bit of time pressure, because the last ferry from the mainland to the island leaves at 16:30.

The only nice thing on the ride: when there are no houses blocking the view, every now and then the pale blue sea and white sandy beach shine through the palm trees.

Then I remember where I'm actually riding my bike right now, and that it's actually rather lovely - if it weren't 50 degrees under my helmet.

While I rest in the shade of a big tree, the honking and squawking carries on merrily around me.

The sight of me sitting in the little wooden hut under the tree, devouring my watermelon, must be hilarious.

At least, plenty of scooters stop a little way off or slow down as they pass. Then watching / gawping, and a honk by way of goodbye, before they rattle off again.

One scooter even pulls up right next to me. "Good afternoon my friend!". I slowly raise my eyes and, to my own surprise, see a Western face.

Instantly I've analysed the gestures and accent, and reply with a smile, in German: "Guten Tag!".

Bullseye!

Eduard is from Austria and lives in Malaysia with his wife. He tips me off to use a cycle path for the next few kilometres. It runs parallel to the noisy main road and is a lot more pleasant.

No sooner said than done. And for a good hour I roll leisurely south along shady paths.

Sooner than I'd like, it's back onto the expressway and back into the old routine: every few kilometres a cool-down stop, a breather, a pep talk. Then fight on.

Three kilometres before my destination comes the final heat collapse.

Luckily I find a kiosk not far from the road. Plenty of ice, an emergency shower with chilled water and a ceiling fan stop me melting any further.

That I miss the last ferry as a result couldn't matter less to me, to put it mildly.

Two litres of water later I ride towards the evening sun at a deliberately leisurely pace, and even find a durian seller by the roadside just before Kota Besut, where I overjoyedly pack my well-earned reward into my pannier.

In Kota Besut I'm greeted by the usual old "friends". Moped taxi drivers who suddenly circle round me, agitated and grinning broadly, bombarding me with the same old questions: "Hey Friend, where from? Where you go? Need Ticket? Need Hotel?" and so on.

Often someone just shouts out loud, so that I jump and sometimes even think someone has actually recognised me. It wouldn't be the first time I'd unexpectedly bumped into old school friends somewhere (preferably from years five to seven). "Blimey, Christian, what are you doing here?!…"

While I get my bearings, at least ten eyes on souped-up two-wheelers are watching me.

Whatever you do, don't show any weakness now! I wave them off with a slight smile. But they keep following me.

The village is small: there are two hotels, a guesthouse, two cafés and the harbour.

As soon as I make even the slightest move towards one of the hotels, one of the drivers is already next to me, talking at me about something (!) and waiting to escort me to the hotel.

I've got no strength left for arguments.

So I calmly look at "Friend 66", smile and say with a slight bow: "Thank you my friend. I'm fine!". He smiles back, wishes me a good evening and rides off.

After that the others, too, leave me in peace, as if under a magic spell.

If you want to recreate this "nuisance", stand next to a bottle bank near you in summer for half an hour and try to shoo away the flies and wasps. The feeling can be intensified by first going for a half-hour jog in a thick cotton tracksuit.

Shortly afterwards I'm lying in my room, whipping the air conditioning down to 24 degrees.

What a day. My eyes are burning and, despite all the effort, I have absolutely no appetite.

As I lie there and the first wave of sleep washes over me, there's a high-pitched whine at my ear. Mosquitoes!

The whole wall is covered with the little bloodsuckers. And they're clever - and far too fast.

Still, I manage to get a few. Then I grab my durian and hastily leave the room for the seafront promenade.

The day is almost unrecognisable: the sun-baked hell has turned into a balmy evening. In the background calm waves murmur, a light breeze blows and the moonlight over the nearby island bathes everything in a silvery light.

A dignified setting for a little durian snack.

Full of anticipation, I find a quiet bench as far from the road as possible and set about ceremonially opening the king of fruits.

But something's missing... That's right: I've barely finished the preparations and opened the fruit slightly when a moped roars up and stops behind my bench. Then silence.

A can of beer is opened. Every now and then there's a loud burp.

Two big Malaysian eyes stare at me out of the darkness for minutes on end, while the mouth slurps greedily at the can.

I carry on as unperturbed as possible and assess the situation out of the corner of my eye. No danger, but whatever you do, don't make direct eye contact!

Too late. Suddenly the man is standing next to me with a friendly "Hello!" and, without being asked, gets stuck straight in.

He wants to help me open the fruit, flaps about between my hands, almost causing some serious cuts, and babbles incomprehensible tips in Malay to himself.

Yes, I know where best to cut a durian! And yes, I also know that durian is spiky - one reason why I'm wearing my gloves!

As there's still no real communication after several attempts, I ask him, in the calmest voice I can manage, to leave.

Crestfallen, he rattles off into the night. Shortly afterwards a group of teenagers storms the nearby playground. They keep their distance, but suddenly talk very loudly and laugh with exaggerated force. The only word I understand: durian. It's possible they're talking about the strange creature on the bench…

It tastes good all the same, but I'm glad when I close the door of my room behind me.

While I'm being devoured by trillions of mosquitoes in the night, I dream that, as the T-1000*, I melt into liquid metal and take on any shape I like.

It's glorious, for example, to keep the roads quiet as a drawing pin under the tyres of my two-wheeled friends.

I'm looking forward to the crossing tomorrow morning and hope for quieter moments on the beach.

## I'll be back
