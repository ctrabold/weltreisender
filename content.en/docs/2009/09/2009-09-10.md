## Thursday, 10 September 2009

## Welcome to the Stone Age

![](/images/2009/09/04/3909446602.jpg)

Thanks to my (admittedly somewhat excessive) consumption of three melons a day (various kinds), my stay in Jalal-Abad stretches to a week.

The watermelons in particular are often treated with a chemical here to make them ripen faster. It causes serious stomach and gut problems - even for the locals. To put it graphically: they become "waterfall melons", and they taste slightly furry and less natural. Sadly you can't tell from the outside, and after weeks without, you only notice the odd taste when it's too late.

![](/images/2009/09/05/3909429922.jpg)

So I spend a good week in Jalal-Abad, always within safe reach of the loo.

As the place also serves as a language school for locals, I get to know lots of Kyrgyz during that time, explore the town and surroundings, and even give two students lessons in English and German. My photo album turns into a lovely picture book ("There! Scarf!... There! Woman!"). I'm sure they'll remember Germany very fondly :)

Despite the trouble, time flies, and I'll remember Jalal-Abad very fondly - above all for the warm welcome from Brother Damian and Brother Vladimir.

![](/images/2009/09/04/3908621129.jpg)

![](/images/2009/09/09/3909435154.jpg)

I also enjoy the best manti so far (dumplings filled with pumpkin or meat), at a place recommended to me by one of the students!

On day 5 I try "TAN" for supper. Somehow the white bottle just smiles at me. The experiment turns out to be a great milk drink that tastes like liquid feta cheese with lots of fizzy water. Just my cup of tea! It's incredibly (!) refreshing and soothes my complaints straight away.

![](/images/2009/09/08/3908649941.jpg)

![](/images/2009/09/08/3908651005.jpg)

My condition stays stable over the next few days, but despite calm, untroubled nights, the ride to Osh starts with jelly legs and a shaky circulation. Luckily the first few kilometres are fairly flat, but I still can't really get going.

![](/images/2009/09/09/3909436806.jpg)

The first climb of the day feels very strange: my legs are like rubber and my pulse is jittery. The nasty headwind forces me to pull my scarf further and further over my face. Luckily the road is in good condition, but it's busy and constantly up and down.

The people are (once again) very "clingy": whistling, calling and bawling after me. As always I try to block it out mentally as best I can, but today it gets increasingly "physical" and aggressive. I feel like a head of cattle being driven along by them.

![](/images/2009/09/09/3908656163.jpg)

Two kids race towards me on their bikes, laughing, cut me up and then - as if nothing had happened - ride alongside me for a while "having a race". Today I have neither the strength nor the nerves for "jokes" like that; my usually well-developed ability to laugh along and smile retreats into the furthest corner, and my mood gets darker and darker.

To match, the weather is very changeable. One moment dark clouds and an ice-cold wind, the next sudden sunshine and a wall of warm air. Sweat pours out of my pores at once, and minutes later carries the cold right through to my bones.

The moment I get my camera out, a horde of little kids comes rushing towards me ("Tourist! Tourist!"). It's like a zombie film - as soon as you've given away your position, they come and get you!

A stop at a petrol station almost ends in a fight when a completely drunk Kyrgyz man paws my bike and obviously wants to knock it over. His friends push him into the kiosk and I make off, fists waving. In another village a shepherd tries to block my front wheel with his stick… Luckily there's no car nearby just then, so I can swerve.

The road is lined with people who get on my nerves and apparently want a piece of me, too. There's hardly a stretch that's uninhabited and lets me take a proper break.

My thighs are rock hard and hurt more every time I want to set off again after a break. It takes minutes for the pain to ease. Everything in my head is cramping up too.

For sheer unfriendliness, these kilometres are by far the worst I've experienced on my trip. The area and the people are aggressive through and through, and by the time the first stone misses my helmet by a whisker and smacks into my handlebar bag, I've only got one thought left: get out of this cursed country!

At no point does the presence of the Kyrgyz make me feel good - more threatened than encouraged - and I just want to get away, away from these country bumpkins, back to civilisation.

The first positive experience of the day is the signpost to Osh, which shows far fewer kilometres than I expect. And indeed, a little later I reach the city.

My hopes of things getting better are already wearing thin at the first set of traffic lights: bumpy roads, dirt, begging children holding out their hands, and after a few metres I get a hearty "F*ck off!" from a black Mercedes, along with the matching hand signal, clearly meant for me. Thanks for that!

I just shake my head and try to laugh. I picture my day from a bird's-eye view. So many grotesque situations in one day - you could almost make a film of it.

In the city I follow the path of least resistance and flow with the traffic somewhere. No idea whether I'm heading for the guesthouse...

At some point I spot a restaurant on the right and eat a portion of manti. I'm frozen through, at the end of my tether. The waitress signals that I have to ride back along the road. Real communication is sadly out of the question, but I try my luck and ride back.

As I roll down the road towards the bazaar, I see a bike ahead of me with a blue bicycle-route sign as a sticker - just like the ones we have in Germany (!!).

With my last ounce of strength I push on the pedals, try to catch up with the cyclist and make myself noticed behind him.

It's Chris from Scotland, who's also on his way to China with his girlfriend. A few minutes later we're sitting in the warm guesthouse together with another couple from Australia / New Zealand.

The kitchen quickly turns into a travel centre. We pore over maps, swap stories from the countries we've been through, and I can finally enjoy the TAN I bought at the petrol station.

My experiences of today leave everyone dismayed. The country gives us all food for thought, and I lie awake until late into the night with my heart racing, thinking over the day.

What a contrast: the guesthouse owner speaks German, is very likeable, and I feel welcome straight away. The accommodation is cheap, central, clean and above all quiet! I've made it! A massive thank you to the cameraman up there!!
