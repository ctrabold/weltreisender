## Sunday, 27 September 2009

## Welcome to China - From Irkeshtam to Kashgar

21 to 27 September - From [Irkeshtam](http://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irkeschtam) to [Kashgar](http://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaschgar)

![](/images/2009/09/20/4031849784.jpg)

My last day in Kyrgyzstan starts with a big breakfast and plenty of tea. Noel and I invested our last som in food and beer yesterday.

There's no wind. The river babbles and the sun is warm. The mountains around us are bathed in pinkish-red light. It's as if Kyrgyzstan wants to give us a particularly pleasant send-off.

![](/images/2009/09/20/4031089851.jpg)

I'm a little worried about the border's "opening hours". Because of the time difference on the Chinese side (Beijing time), the Chinese side goes on lunch break shortly after the Kyrgyz side opens. Hopefully we'll get there in time.

![](/images/2009/09/20/4031092379.jpg)

We're optimistic and then roll over the hill to the border at 60 km/h. Watched by dozens of lorry drivers, we coast in calmly and pass the 2nd, 3rd and finally the 4th Kyrgyz checkpoint (we passed the 1st checkpoint 20 kilometres back). The same things are checked at every checkpoint: passport, entry and exit stamps, visa. Average time spent per checkpoint: 20 minutes, including small talk with the officials.

![](/images/2009/09/20/4019094040.jpg)

After that there's a small, narrow, dusty climb past another queue of lorries, until a sign saying "Welcome in China" tells us we're on Chinese soil. Silent cheering, because the atmosphere is stiff and cold. Marked by military drill, dust masks and lorry noise. Completely different from the Kyrgyz side.

A hand waves us over from the passport hut. The moment our passports are in Chinese hands, an official ambushes us with a bellow and chases us back in front of the Welcome sign.

"First Check Luggage, then Passport!!!" he explains, and we have to open all our bags ("You open - I see!!"). Every piece of kit has to be explained. Several times the wind blows our papers away, which the official couldn't care less about.

Then it's on to the 2nd Chinese border post. We quickly push our bikes up the slippery steps into the building, fill in the immigration card, have our temperature taken and our luggage X-rayed, passport checked once more...

As I'm putting my bags back on the bike, I'm surprised to find I'm alone. Where's Noel? His first temperature reading was positive, the second one OK. There was some discussion, which is why it took a bit longer for him.

Then we're waved out of the building - quick quick quick! The officials' stomachs are rumbling and we are - just in time for the lunch break - really and truly in China! Right after us, the border doors are locked.

We're overjoyed and celebrate our smooth entry into the new country with a big plate of lagman right in the border town of Sumhana. This time not quietly, but with genuinely authentic slurping and other Chinese table noises ([pictures](#pictures))

![](/images/2009/09/21/4031104985.jpg)

Verdict: the best lagman so far! Completely new taste buds are stimulated. I'm looking forward to the new cuisine, even though the menu is full of unfamiliar characters. No English translation, nothing!

Happily, next to the restaurant there's also a bit of food to buy. With hands and feet we bargain for fruit, vegetables and water for the onward journey. The seller laughs and gives us an onion and a melon for the road. Thank you!

The change of country is huge: brown mountains, perfect roads, new script and no more Russian. The signs are still bilingual: before it was Cyrillic and Latin script, now it's Arabic and Chinese script.

We don't know any of these languages. So zero orientation at first and a certain uncertainty. Luckily the road runs dead straight towards Ulugqat, so we can ignore the signs for now. Still, an interesting feeling.

At over 75 km/h we race through the new, clay-brown landscape. It's up and down. Before dark the tent is up behind piles of sand at the roadside. A few metres further on, lorries rumble past. So our first night in China is pretty noisy. (

When we reach the first village I'm especially curious about the people. How will they react to these strange people on bikes?

It's astonishing: the people seem to have changed along with the border. No more wild shrieking, no more unfriendly "Hey Tourist! Tourist!". Instead, friendly smiles and quiet waves. Brilliant! Thank you!

The rest of the route is marked by interesting rock formations, mud huts, camels and the Red River. The tailwind is strong and together we race down the mountains towards Kashgar at top speed ([pictures](#pictures)).

Before Kashgar the road becomes a real motorway (![](/images/2009/09/23/autobahn-nach-kashgar_4019131094_o.jpg)).

The many checkpoints thin out the traffic nicely, so we have the carriageway almost to ourselves. Riding is great fun. So much space!

Sadly, in Kashgar city there are several hairy moments with moped riders. Most mopeds have hybrid engines and run on electric power in town. They only splutter into life on hills. Combined with what is (to us) extremely reckless driving, that's a real shock - and damned unfamiliar, and therefore dangerous.

I wish I had an extra-loud horn, but I can't find one anywhere.

Yes, the big city overwhelms us (locals will smile at this). Our first attempts to ask locals for directions fail completely. They make us even more disorientated.

We follow our instincts and shortly afterwards find a bookshop, where I buy a map and bump into Ron again. We'd met in Bishkek. Noel had also got to know him in Georgia.

Ron takes us to his hotel. At the feast that follows we celebrate our arrival and our reunion.

The next day we move to the cheaper youth hostel at the other end of town, where we want to spend a good week before heading out into the great wide desert.
