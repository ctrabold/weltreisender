## Saturday, 29 August 2009

## The Last Pass Is No Laughing Matter

![](/images/2009/08/29/3884269566.jpg)

For the last few days I've lived mainly on a loaf of bread and water. Only rarely were there any shops in the settlements. Biscuits are the culinary highlight.

![](/images/2009/08/28/3883962346.jpg)

On the pass between Song-Köl and Kazarman I meet Mario and his American girlfriend Sheri coming the other way. Together we find a camping spot hidden from view and pitch our tents in front of a glorious mountain view.

Today, for the first time in ages, there's properly cooked food: pasta with tomato sauce! DELICIOUS! Thank you for that!

The two of them are cycling to the Philippines and want to get married there next year.

> Wishing you a good journey and a lovely honeymoon!

Luckily I still had energy bars from Germany, otherwise things would have got tight on the mountain. This stretch of the trip is the hardest thing I've ever done on a bike. And the next big pass, the Kazarman or Kaldama Pass at over 3,000 metres, is still to come.

We're all horrified by the state of the roads, talk until late into the night and count shooting stars. Otherwise it's absolutely silent. Mario assures me the road gets better.

Over the next few days I battle through desert-like landscapes and, just before the pass, find the campsite by the river where I stay for two nights. Then it's up.

I'm really nervous. After just a few metres my pulse shoots up unpleasantly. My stomach makes itself heard and I feel rotten again.

It's almost a relief to see a storm brewing in the distance. I escape into the nearest field of flowers, pitch the tent and sleep like a log while the rain drums on the tent. Two hours later I'm woken by hot flushes. The sun is blazing down again.

I want to conquer the pass today - so I ride on, though every muscle fibre is crying out for rest.

![](/images/2009/08/29/3883462483.jpg)

The road really does get better - or rather more even, with a firmer surface. It leads through an enchanted valley with abandoned houses and rickety bridges; often the mountain river washes the road away.

![](/images/2009/08/29/3883471593.jpg)

Fuelled by energy gels, I whip myself up metre by metre. From halfway up, towers of dust metres high come at me before every bend. As I round the corner, hurricane-force wind hits me in the face. I'm not ready for it, and I simply fall over, Rondolf and all.

From now on this (serious) little game repeats itself at every bend. With a tailwind I can sometimes even ride up the climb in fourth gear. With a headwind it's pushing and shouting on the inside of the bend - because the wind could easily blow you down into the valley.

Metre by metre I get closer to the pass. My gaze is fixed, at times cross-eyed. The altitude is really getting to me today.

The sky gets darker and darker, the electricity pylons groan menacingly in the wind, and dust pelts against my ski goggles, which I packed specially for extreme situations like this. Anything else would just be blown off my face.

Luckily the last stretch is fairly sheltered from the wind, so I make good, safe progress. On the pass itself there's hardly any time for romantic photos. There's not much to see apart from clouds anyway, and as I reach the top, thunder starts rumbling behind me. Let's get out of here!

Luckily Mario turns out to be right. Driven on by the monster cloud above me, I tear down the descent in the dusk and hurtle past the shouting, whistling yurts at almost 40 km/h.

Soon afterwards I find a safe camping spot under an old birch tree. It smells wonderfully of wood and herbs. The tent is barely up when it starts pouring. Lightning and thunder rip through the night. The power lines above me give me cover.

I've been really lucky today! From now on it's downhill towards Jalal-Abad, I mumble, and fall into a deep sleep.
