## Monday, 3 August 2009

## That Good Thunderstorm Feeling

![](/images/2009/08/03/3832609269.jpg)

It takes a good four hours to get a receipt for my room. The good lady at reception stubbornly refuses to give me the receipt without a registration.

You need to know that in Russia you have to register within three days if you stay in one place. Since that doesn't apply to me, I don't need a registration. It wasn't a problem at any of the other gostinitsas either. Just today, everything's going wrong.

Her colleague makes things even worse with her hysterical laughter.

![](/images/2009/08/03/3833357218.jpg)

Thanks to a young Russian woman who speaks very good German and translates, it all gets cleared up and I can finally set off.

For a long time I ride with a tailwind on wonderfully smooth roads without seeing a single car. The first car pulls up next to me on a farm track in the middle of huge cornfields.

When I explain where I'm heading, the driver tells me I'm completely on the wrong track. In his view this road doesn't go there, and I'd better turn back.

So back I go along those lovely kilometres. This time into a headwind and uphill.

![](/images/2009/08/03/3833365880.jpg)

The road I'm looking for turns out to be a real motorway of a farm track. The surface is packed so hard that it's even better to ride on than the road! The tailwind sweeps me through the fields like a wooden roller coaster (wooden because it's just as smooth and fast as the one at Heide Park, the theme park in northern Germany).

Looking out across the fields I can see a dark bank of cloud full of huge flashes of lightning. I stop and watch. Good thing the tailwind is pushing the trouble ahead of me, I think - and then I notice that the wind has changed direction.

The thunder comes closer and closer and the air gets thick, the way I know from earlier storms. A really fantastic, strange atmosphere. Still, this time I'd like to stay dry, and I search the open country frantically for a suitable spot.

Choosing a pitch means weighing up the risk of lightning against shelter from unwanted visitors. A few kilometres on I find a nice clump of trees with a patch of grass in the middle.

There I pitch my tent in record time, chuck the panniers under the flysheet and quickly gather some dry wood. Moments later the first heavy drops come pelting down, and I'm pleasantly cooled off by the time I crawl into the tent.

Contented, I enjoy my supper and fall asleep to the chirping of crickets and the fading rumble of thunder. Spot on!
