## Thursday, 27 August 2009

## Wholegrain Biscuits at 3,000 Metres

![](/images/2009/08/26/3884178434.jpg)

The last two days were absolutely awful. Rarely on this trip have I felt so alone, so trapped and so at the mercy of things.

Completely drained, I battle for hours against a brutal headwind across the wasteland around Song-Köl lake, past yurts where money-grabbing Kyrgyz lie in wait for tourists. The mountain panorama around the lake bores me.

I just want to get away, and above all _down_! At well over 3,000 metres, after the effort of the climb with my heart rate above 160 bpm and more than 500 metres higher than last night's camp, I'm worried whether my body will cope with the strain. The descent has to be somewhere!

On top of that, in the middle of nowhere I get my second puncture of the day. The roads are too much for the Marathon XR. This morning a little piece of wire bored its way in, and now there's a textbook snakebite - a hole on each side of the tyre.

A one-eyed Kyrgyz farmer keeps me "company" and watches every move I make. I jump when he starts singing a traditional song on top of it all. My mood is icy, like the wind that's been swirling around me for days. I can hardly laugh any more; I think of flying home, a warm bed, feeling safe.

I quickly fit a brand-new rear tyre. Just get out of here. But the cold has made the new tyre stiff, and as I'm pulling it on I tear the side of my thumbnail deep. Now I'm bleeding too!

The old man helps me kindly and I'm soon able to carry on. A few metres further on, a woman waves to me. I hesitate, then ride up to her yurt after all and, by the warm stove, enjoy sweet black butter tea, [kumis](http://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumis) and flatbread with a delicious fatty dip! What a relief after all that hardship, because it tastes DELICIOUS!

The [kumis](http://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumis) (fermented mare's milk) is a bit of an acquired taste, a bit like liquid kefir. Even more sour, with more of an "animal aroma". You really must try it, because it's very refreshing! It agreed with me just fine!

After that I ride on, a little stronger. It's flat and straight all the way. A yurt here and there, right and left. Otherwise horses, sheep and goats. And dull, endless space. A thunderstorm is brewing ahead of me and I hurriedly pitch my tent. The tent is barely up when a child's voice squeals "Tourist!" from the hill above me. I hate it!

Shortly afterwards Father and Mother appear, looking at me rather darkly and making it clear that I'd better come to their yurt. I accept the hospitable offer and take down my tent - the groundsheet tears and almost blows away, the storm is that strong.

When I reach the yurt, the man lends a hand. Before I can blink - and above all before I can ask about the price - my luggage is inside the yurt. He beats about the bush for an answer until his wife finally speaks up and demands 375 som.

That seems too much for a camping spot, and I make it clear that I only have 200 som. The woman won't budge, and I move on in a daze.

Luckily it's only spitting a little, and 10 kilometres further on, completely spent, I reach another yurt settlement. There they offer me a camping spot for 100 som, which seems fairer.

I've barely parked Rondolf when I'm ushered into the guest yurt. I can pitch the tent later, the man assures me - they're waiting for me with dinner!

Although I'm not hungry at all, I accept the supposed invitation and step into the warm room, where a raucous party is in full swing.

Four Kyrgyz men and a woman are sitting at the laid table and give me a warm welcome. A babble of languages crashes over me. The situation completely overwhelms me, and only now do I realise how chilled I am.

Shivering, I spoon up the soup, which has a roughly chopped piece of spine in it. Meanwhile I answer questions as best I can and slurp my second kumis of the day. This one has no herbs in it and tastes completely different again.

When the vodka is handed round, the atmosphere gets more and more intense. I hardly get a chance to eat, I'm hugged, I fear for my belongings, but in the end everything stays peaceful.

The lads are well and truly sloshed and invite me to sleep in the yurt with them. Outside, a nasty storm with lightning and thunder is sweeping across the land and it's pitch dark.

In these conditions I don't fancy pitching a tent, so I make myself as comfortable as I can in the yurt with the others. The yurt is packed and we sleep crammed together.

The stove stinks more than it warms, and there's burping and farting galore. I get cold and gradually feel thoroughly miserable.

When, shortly before 1 am, my neighbour's burping and gulping turns into full-on throwing up, my heart pounds even harder against my chest. I struggle to keep my composure and to get some fresh air. I've never heard noises like it. The whole lot lands right next to the bed, INSIDE the yurt.

The only question at that moment: when will the stench come? The typical smell of butyric acid doesn't come; instead it smells of half-digested mutton, mare's milk and faeces.

Half asleep, at 3 am I hear the green bile trying to make its way out again. Sleep is out of the question now, and I seriously consider doing a runner without paying.

But my tiredness and good manners stop me from fleeing early, and so I get to enjoy the breakfast spew at 6 am as well - this time IN FRONT OF the tent.

When I leave, they want 400 som. For a night like that and a soup that was badly short of salt.

I feel ripped off and really rotten. For the first few metres on the bumpy track I retch at every pothole. My clothes stink of that night for a long time afterwards, and the sound of that long, miserable retching follows me far down into the valley.

> Stay away from Russian / Kyrgyz vodka. I've heard from lots of travellers that even a few glasses can cause the nastiest problems, up to two days of throwing up!

My mood has now finally hit rock bottom. If one more Kyrgyz gives me any grief, it'll get physical. For kilometres, dark thoughts rage through my head. This country makes me sick - quite literally.

The children at the roadside are getting on my nerves too; their whistling goes right over my head and I race off as fast as I can. The final climb to the pass is short and brutal. Thanks to several breaks I still make it to the top in one piece and can hardly believe it.

The view is gigantic, but a jeep spoils it. When a Kyrgyz man with a camera phone gets out as well, my fists clench and I turn away. He's the last thing I need right now!

But it stays quiet. In the distance I even hear German voices. Now I'm completely losing it, I tell myself, and turn round. It really is two women from Germany, exploring the country with a Kyrgyz guide (wise decision!). I can't help smiling.

![](/images/2009/08/26/3883350775.jpg)

We soon get chatting and they cheer me up by every trick in the book. As a parting gift I get a packet of VitalGebäck (wholegrain biscuits)! With sunflower seeds!! From Germany!!!

**Thank you for these delicious biscuits! They've sweetened every pass for me so far!**

As the jeep turns the corner, my worries have blown away and I can really enjoy the glorious view.

![](/images/2009/08/26/3884184982.jpg)

The landscape changes completely again. Trees and lush greenery line the road. A clear stream bubbles along in the middle, and I find a dream campsite where I can patch my tyres and sleep very peacefully for two nights.
