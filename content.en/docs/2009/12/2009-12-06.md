## Sunday, 6 December 2009

To Xichang in Wet St Nicholas Boots

​3 to 6 December

The last few days in a nutshell: hardcore!

Through miserable weather (grey, cold and wet) we cross the first foothills of the Tibetan Plateau ([http://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hochland_von_Tibet](http://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hochland_von_Tibet)).

And (as so often) it all starts out relaxed and harmless: I reach Ebian - the town with the missing D - fresh and cheerful. Despite the long break from cycling, it's going well. A hotel is quickly found.

The traffic chaos in front of the hotel isn't a one-off jam - as I first assumed - but chronic. Apparently the entire town's traffic squeezes through this alley. The road is hopelessly gridlocked and everyone is honking like mad.

I've searched long and hard and finally found it: here, right here in front of the hotel, lies the epicentre of China's honking madness. It doesn't get any worse than this! The narrow alley between the houses concentrates the noise even more and makes it EVEN more painful. I can't understand how anyone can stand in this street for more than a minute.

Particularly handy: the room faces right onto the street and the window always stays open a crack - the fitter obviously got his measurements wrong. At any rate, I can't close it completely. So the night is loud, full of exhaust fumes and cold.

Even so, the first day back on the bike goes smoothly and well. The road and the scenery are OK. Sadly, the hazy weather spoils many lovely views into the valley.

The next morning, after a quick breakfast, we climb from 700 metres above sea level to 3,000. Time and again the road drops down to the river below 1,000 metres and then climbs back up, usually at the end of little villages, where small children or macho moped riders can easily latch on to our back wheel.

If there's one thing I've learnt on my trip, it's "ignoring" - or rather "optimising tunnel vision". It's not perfect yet, but often good enough.

Friday, which started out so harmless and relaxed, turns out to be a really tough nut to crack - in the "one of the hardest stages" category.

Our destination for the day, "Hongxi", just won't come any closer. Always one more bend, then one more climb - all in pitch-black night.

The little clusters of houses by the roadside are eerily quiet and dark. And suddenly, out of nowhere: mud track. The tarmac disappears and big, slippery stones make every metre a struggle. From here on, it's time to get off and push.

After more than an hour of pushing, my wrists are aching. The mud on the tyres gets stickier and stickier. The thermometer shows sub-zero temperatures - it's freezing!

Combined with freezing rain and deep puddles, every metre becomes a bigger challenge. My shoes are wet, but luckily my feet stay dry.

On the left a sheer rock face, on the right, after two or three metres, a steep drop - or so I assume, because visibility is under 10 metres.

After two hours, slight optical illusions make it hard to keep my bearings. Suddenly the view to the left seems clear again. But on closer inspection it turns out to be snow, which looks very different in the pale light.

Despite the adverse conditions I'm fairly calm and confident that the day will end well. Eventually we find room for our tent in a little lay-by and fall into a deep sleep just before two o'clock.

At four a violent storm sweeps over the tent. Anxious minutes. Did we push the tent pegs in firmly enough? Yes, it stays standing.

The next morning we see our surroundings properly for the first time. In the weak sunlight, the drop looks a good deal closer than we'd thought.

The wind has blown away most of the fog, and as a small consolation there's a lovely view into the valley. The summit is visible too!

For the last kilometre, in the shadow of the mountain, we can only inch forward. The road is as slippery as glass and extra steep once again.

When the road levels out, mind and body relax too - we've made it!

I trundle slowly down the mountain along the edge of the road. I keep having to stop, because the new, frozen landscape with its eerie high fog is wonderfully bizarre.

At some point the sun comes out and shows the gloom who's boss. In no time the weather, the road and my mood all change.

After a few metres the bike and the road are dry, and the last lumps of ice practically evaporate or drop off the bike in disgust. At last I can "bomb" down the mountain, Kyrgyz-style.

In fact, the whole scenario reminds me a lot of the Kyrgyz stages (even the people's "annoyance factor" is about the same).

In the valley the surface turns back to tarmac and gets more boooring, but faster, and a good half hour later I reach the next village.

At around 4 pm we reach Hongxi and decide to take a room at the local "hotel". The hot shower is cold, and out in the corridor the Chinese are celebrating their weekend. It's loud and uncomfortable the whole time.

At 6 in the morning a guest hawks up so loudly in the corridor that I think he's about to throw up against my door. Unpleasant. Unnecessary.

And we've picked out a special treat for tomorrow, Sunday: we want to ride all the way to Xichang in one go. A good 150 kilometres - a long stage with a demanding profile.

At 8 o'clock we're in the saddle, pedalling away.

Most of the way, the road winds prettily downhill, downstream towards Meigu. (Important: "downstream" does not mean "only downhill"! There may well be the odd punchy climb in there.)

The first forty kilometres are very fast and easy. Sadly the sun is hiding behind thick clouds. From Meigu onwards it's exactly the other way round: now it's up and down, upstream towards Zhaojue. The road gets narrow and worse. But the sun is blazing. A fair swap.

What's particularly grating, though, is the constant honking again.

Small and big buses, 4x4 monsters, small cars, mopeds and three-wheelers - they all honk. Before every bend, after every bend, before every hill, after every hill, before every overtaking manoeuvre, during every overtaking manoeuvre, and quite happily just because, to let you know they're "there". You never know what might happen.

The green-and-white minibuses (village taxis) deserve a special mention. One of these things is forever overtaking me, and 80 per cent of the time at least one head is hanging out of the window, throwing up, having thrown up or getting ready for the next round.

Most buses have light-brown vomit stains under the windows on both sides. There are often little piles and splashes on the road. Carelessness is punished: look down into the valley a moment too long and you hear "schwiff schwiff schwiff" under your tyre. Bull's-eye!

That is NOT funny, because with all the bends and the bus drivers' very aggressive driving, anyone can get hit! Especially if you're riding nice and slowly at the right-hand edge of the road.

Otherwise the route is boring. The villages unfriendly and loud. We stop only once, for a breakfast of noodle soup. Otherwise liquid nourishment is the order of the day.

At the end of the day I somehow find myself on a 3,300-metre pass, enjoying the evening sun as it sets behind the Himalayas. Amazing!

It's another 40 to 50 kilometres to Xichang, but this time it's all lovely and downhill - or so the logical conclusion goes. After all, Xichang is on a river, so lower down.

This time we decide not to camp but to see the plan through. There's bound to be a hotel in town, and if all goes well we'll be there by around 9 pm.

So we roll downhill - and after a few hundred metres, a shock: it's going steeply uphill again. Oh no!

Wasn't that the end of the line after all? Yes, it was! That was the last climb. From here on it's downhill all the way. And how.

With every light and reflector switched on, we bomb down the road. The descent takes hours and demands enormous concentration. Even with a good dynamo, you only ever see a small patch straight ahead. The head torch is barely enough to light up the bends properly.

On top of that, the road is in bad shape and we often have to swerve around stones and potholes. Luckily there's hardly any traffic, but the few oncoming headlights are enough to "blind" us for a moment each time and make us slam on the brakes.

Otherwise it's pitch black. Really no light at all apart from ours. Which makes the starry sky all the more impressive! The Milky Way close enough to touch. Despite being a bit pressed for time, we stop twice to enjoy the silence and the darkness. Hard to take in. Beautiful despite - or precisely because of - the hard work.

Just before 10 pm we check into our warm hotel room in Xichang. The shower is great, and next door there's a restaurant and some shops.

We ask at reception how best to get to the station, then fall asleep.

In the morning we ride to the station, I buy two tickets for the next train, and we hand in our bikes and heavy luggage. Everything runs like clockwork. With only the bare essentials we then head back into town and look around.

The town is unspectacular, so we spend our time there with a clear conscience on breakfast and various lunches. Shortly after 8 pm we board the train, settle into our hard-sleeper compartment (2 x 3 bunk beds) and soon fall asleep.
