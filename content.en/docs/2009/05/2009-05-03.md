## Sunday, 3 May 2009

Air - the enemy of packing

I always thought liquids and heavy things were what you absolutely had to avoid when packing. And that's true. But what I find much worse is: air!

It creeps into every last nook and cranny, and the waterproof (and therefore airtight) panniers turn it into a real problem: they puff up for no reason and waste an incredible amount of space. Even with more breathable bags there's always an air bubble, although I've tried plenty of stuffing and packing techniques.

Getting the air out of the bags is a seriously annoying test of patience anyway, and squeezing them so that really only the contents are left inside is practically impossible. By the time the bags are in the actual bike pannier at the latest, the air is back.

A useful invention here is the compression sack, which, to be honest, I'm only now starting to appreciate (before, I'd rashly dismissed them as a gimmick / marketing ploy).

It turns out these sacks really are extremely handy for keeping the air out when packing and making better use of the space in the pannier.

How do compression sacks work?

You've got a fairly ordinary-looking sack that you fill with clothes as usual (solid objects don't work). What's special about it is the little valve at the bottom and the airtight material.

Once the sack is packed, you open the little valve at the bottom so the air can escape. It works best if you kneel down and hold the sack between your legs. Now you roll the top fold over three or four times. Then you can press down on the sack with both hands and squeeze out loooots of air.

You keep going until there's no more hissing from the valve. Then you quickly close the valve with one hand and seal the sack.

The contents are now tightly compressed and much more compact than before. Of course there's a small downside too: if the sack has a hole, the contents stay puffed up, compression sack or not. If you've already packed the pannier to the limit, that can lead to space problems. For emergencies, the sacks come with a repair patch.

We'll see how the compression sacks hold up in practice. So far I'm pretty impressed.
