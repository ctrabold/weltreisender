## Saturday, 14 November 2009

Green, green, green is all I see

Emei / Emeishan, 9 to 14 November

Pictures:
A hot shower at the hotel before bed clears my head and leaves my legs pleasantly heavy.

I sleep so well that the next morning I decide to stay another day and explore the area around the magical "Emei". There are signs to natural landmarks everywhere. All I can actually see, though, is grey fog.

My voyage of discovery begins in the nearby internet café. It's just after 10 in the morning and the place is packed with girls chatting online. From every corner comes the familiar blip of chat programmes. The lads are presumably still sleeping off last night's gaming battles.

I soon track down a youth hostel nearby, and a few minutes later I'm having breakfast at Teddy Bear Youth Hostel. The atmosphere is fine and, at 2 EUR, the room is a lot cheaper than the hotel (10 EUR).

There's plenty going on, and in the evening I meet Dennis from Kiel, Germany, Ethan from the USA and his younger brother from Israel. Together we head off on a "night-snacking" tour: a high-energy meal at one of the town's many deep-fried food stalls (pictures: [pictures](#pictures)).

Over a cold beer we decide to climb Mount Emei together the next day.

The weather is mild. Even so, I'm expecting snow on the summit, which is over 3,000 metres high, so I pack some warm layers.

It turns into a tough two-day trek. The path to the top is almost entirely steps. With all the damp, it's very slippery in places. Still, we take plenty of breaks to enjoy the scenery, because it's indescribably romantic, lush and green... just wonderful!

Towards evening a storm blows in, and we're hugely relieved to find shelter in a Buddhist monastery.

There we meet Christina from Switzerland and Steve from Hawaii. The five of us share a room and have by far the funniest night since my trip began.

Ethan has the bright idea of buying two little bottles of Beijo ([http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baijiu](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baijiu)) [YES, revolting!] and a pack of cards as a nightcap. The plan works: within minutes we're playing Swiss card games and Israeli children's games, and every so often we're rolling around on the beds in fits of laughter.

The wind batters the simple wooden roof of the hut and the draught finds every crack. The "freezing cold" of the dry desert takes on a whole new meaning here: this damp cold chills you to the bone. Luckily the beds have electric blankets, so we manage to sleep a bit better.

After a good breakfast (very basic, yet - or perhaps because of that - one of the tastiest so far) we carry on to the summit. The temperature hovers around freezing. The water on the leaves has frozen overnight. A photographer's paradise!

With every metre we climb, the drizzle turns icier, and at the summit we're met by a proper blizzard. The wind is razor-sharp and laced with ice crystals. Visibility is well under 15 metres. Within minutes we lose each other on the viewing platform.

Sadly, the famous golden statue is little more than a vague outline. But you can still feel it. Its presence is impressive, and for a moment the hundreds of Chinese tourists around us are forgotten.

On the way up we'd already agreed that walking back down in these conditions would be no fun. Our shoes are soaked, our noses are frozen, and there's only one thought in our heads: to the Hot Springs!

The Hot Springs are fantastic! There's hot, very hot and boiling hot water, massages, and even a sauna!

After more than six months without a swim, I let myself soak until I'm thoroughly waterlogged.

In the Chinese sauna I show the others what a proper German-style _Aufguss_ is, then - completely dried out - enjoy an hour with the masseur, who finally kneads my knotted cyclist's muscles back into shape.

To finish, I head back to the steaming main pool where almost everyone has gathered. In the light drizzle, a good 50 Chinese are standing around in the hot water, eating sausages and/or popcorn and chatting away. I'm the only Westerner. It's glorious!

By the exit I come across a "fish therapy" pool, and shortly afterwards I'm letting hundreds of tiny fish nibble away at me. It's simply wonderful. I've rarely felt so at one with nature. The Chinese, sadly, are far less sentimental about it. Just as I'm drifting in the pool, utterly relaxed, four Chinese men storm in, squealing and shrieking at every little nibble and ruining any chance of relaxation. Still: definitely give it a go!

I sleep like a log and wake up the next morning a new man. It's Friday, the others say their goodbyes and head off to Chengdu - and I'm on my own again. It's a strange feeling every time. Being alone again after such a cheerful bunch is hard.

After endless dithering, I decide to extend my visa in Chengdu rather than Leshan. Leshan is supposed to process it within two working days, but it's the weekend, and I'd really like to either move on OR see the others again. Not least because the World Cyber Games, the world championship of computer gaming, are taking place in Chengdu on Sunday.

Still half asleep, I catch the express bus to Chengdu early on Saturday morning and arrive two hours later. Rondolf, my bike, stays behind at the hostel. I plan to come back in a week and then carry on to Kunming via Leshan.

The moment I roll into Chengdu I feel at home. I quickly find the hostel and soak up the warm welcome over a doughnut breakfast.

Fingers crossed everything goes to plan on Monday!
