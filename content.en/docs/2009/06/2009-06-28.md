## Sunday, 28 June 2009

Patched Up and Sewn Up

Until breakfast at 7:30 I spend two hours tidying up and packing.

The room looks like a drying chamber. My jersey and rain jacket are hanging in the wardrobe.

In the left-hand corner my rain trousers are spread out over the armchair, with the overshoes in front of it, turned inside out. On the right, over the chair and desk, cycling shorts, money belt, arm warmers and gloves. In between, panniers, the white-and-brown marbled hotel towels and cycling shoes stuffed with a Polish local rag.

Despite all my efforts, the things are still damp. As a small consolation it looks friendlier outside than yesterday. With quiet hope I go down to breakfast. When I put on my normal cycling gear afterwards, drizzle is hitting the window. So: rain gear back on.

After a quick chat with the lady at reception (where from, where to, weather) I step out of the building into intense humidity.

My sweat pores open immediately and warm wetness trickles down my back. Under the roof of the campsite showers I patch both tubes. Two Polish cyclists watch me with interest.

As it turns out, the two holes have nothing to do with each other. The second hole is considerably smaller than no. 1 and was probably caused by a trapped grain of sand. Shortly afterwards Rondolf is rolling again, and with an uneasy feeling I ride to the nearby "Auschwitz Museum".

The entrance is extremely busy. Dozens of coaches are arriving or leaving. I park Rondolf at an information sign and shortly afterwards I'm surrounded by the blocks of the concentration camp.

Despite the many tourists, I'm suddenly completely alone again. My initial uncertainty about whether my cycling and rain gear is appropriate quickly vanishes when I see the other visitors: garish little skirts, high heels and reckless use of mobile phones and cameras. Even so, I walk quietly through this place, which stays with me for a long, long time.

Just before 2pm I set off again. As I'm about to leave, a Spaniard leaps towards me from the side and we chat for a moment about our travel plans. After he's got on the shuttle bus to Auschwitz-Birkenau, the sun suddenly bursts out from behind the clouds and it gets boiling hot.

## _¡Que grande! ¡Gracias amigo!_

I tear off my rain gear and enjoy riding on in short, airy clothing. For a moment I watch the Spaniard go and wonder whether I shouldn't see the rest of it too.

I deliberately decide against visiting Auschwitz-Birkenau. I feel it's time to move on now and process what I've seen. "I'll come back here," I tell myself, and set off with mixed feelings.

On the one hand I'm absolutely thrilled that the sun is shining and my damp things can finally dry out properly again; on the other, the images I've just seen are pounding in my head. I concentrate on the route, which is unspectacular apart from two river crossings ("unpack!").

The daily item "find somewhere to stay" then ruins my day again.

Just as I realise there's no accommodation to be found for miles around, the thunderstorm I'd hoped would pass me by comes down. Within seconds I'm dripping wet again.

When I notice, I'm in the middle of trying to find out from a security guard where the nearest guesthouse is. It's in Chrzanów, 11 kilometres away - where exactly, I'd have to ask again when I get there. After almost 50 kilometres and 3 hours, I'm longing for rest.

Instead, things get really serious now. It's getting dark, the country road is busy and lined with puddles and potholes. In moments like these it's incredibly hard to keep your nerve. Slight panic sets in.

Again the only way out is forward, so I race along the wet country road. At my destination I ask a young couple for directions. They escort me to the hotel, where I'm welcomed warmly (and in German). Once the formalities are sorted, I fall into a long, deep sleep.
