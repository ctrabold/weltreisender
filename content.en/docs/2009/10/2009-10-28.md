## Wednesday, 28 October 2009

Desert Plans - From Kashgar to Xining

27 September to 28 October

The city's bazaar is boring and naff, and the visit to the livestock market sobering ([pictures]({{< relref "/tags/kashgar" >}})). There are almost more tourists than animals to gawp at. On top of that, the way the animals are treated is strange - but every visitor should be aware of that before going. Anyway, I'm glad to be back so I can wash the dung off my shoes and get my things ready for departure.

The youth hostel has a washing machine and a big shower room, so I was able to clean the bike there (No, the shower did NOT get any dirtier because of it! It's amazing how little the bathrooms get cleaned here).

The bike and my clothes are spotless, the chain freshly oiled and the tyres pumped up. I'm ready to go.

Pictures from Kashgar: [Kashgar]({{< relref "/tags/kashgar" >}})

On Monday, 28 September, we set off towards Yengisar, the first sizeable town on the southern route through the Taklamakan Desert. Unlike when we arrived, we quickly find our way out of the city, but we're turned back at the first checkpoint. The southern route is closed to foreign cyclists. We can either carry on by car or bus. Or take the northern route...

But we don't give up that easily and try to get round the checkpoint ([pictures]({{< relref "/tags/kashgar" >}})), which works nicely. Sadly, after 70 kilometres another checkpoint is waiting.

There they explain that we can't go any further and have to go back to Kashgar.

After a few unsuccessful attempts to explain ourselves (too far, sleep, dark!) we turn round and try to sneak past the checkpoint unnoticed via a narrow bridge.

In the next village we take a break, eat a decent portion of lagman and wonder about the big crowd gathering round our bikes. As we're slurping the last noodles, two men signal to us to follow them into the police building. Dismayed, we set off with them.

I fear the worst, try to remember the phone number of the German embassy, and am surprised when the first thing we're given is a big plate of melon slices.

The officers are very friendly. Slowly my fears evaporate. Long minutes later the boss appears, along with a note-taker, and questions us about the purpose of our stay. Passports are inspected. More questions are asked.

We're in a restricted area and have to leave as quickly as possible. Staying is only possible with a special permit, which takes at least three days to process.

Again and again we stress that we absolutely have to get to Yengisar as quickly as possible - not Kashgar! - which is eventually understood. To make sure we get out of the area safely, we're given a police escort to the edge of town.

And so we follow the police car out to a field somewhere between Kashgar and Yengisar. Once there, we get them to write us a permit saying we're allowed to ride as far as Yengisar. The permit is written in two minutes. We thank them with a firm handshake and set off into the unknown.

It's already dark when we give up looking for a hotel and escape into a field on the left. The road is lined with maize fields that might make a nice campsite.

As we're about to pitch our tents, we spot a farmer and ask his permission. He says no and takes us to his house, where we eat together and also find a place to sleep.

Grateful and exhausted, we fall asleep, until shortly after 1 am loud knocking wakes us. The police are standing in the hut and we have to leave immediately. Our bikes are roughly stuffed into a small bus and we drive with two policemen to Yengisar - or rather to just before Yengisar, because when another checkpoint appears in the dark, the driver brakes hard and tells us to ride on from here.

The bikes are barely unloaded when the car roars off with squealing tyres. Freezing, we stand in the pitch-black night in the middle of the desert in front of a Chinese checkpoint. Unsure whether we'll get through this time, we wonder whether it's a clever idea to ride past the guards without lights.

We decide to ride on with our lights on, as if it were the most natural thing in the world, which works perfectly. The guards greet us cheerfully and we pass the checkpoint without any trouble.

My heart is pounding with joy and I try to enjoy my first night ride of the trip. The starry sky is magical, the temperatures bearable, and a short while later the lights of Yengisar appear.

Once there, we look for a hotel, which at this time of night is very, very difficult. Background: as a foreign tourist you're only allowed to stay in certain (tourist) hotels. These are usually fenced off and guarded.

So under these circumstances there are at least two extra barriers to overcome before you get to a warm bed:

1. Wake up the security guard
2. Wake up the receptionist
   Often there's a third point too:
3. Convince the receptionist that you HAVE to take a room here, because there's nowhere else to sleep in town.

That's usually the hardest part.

Luckily we meet a Chinese man on the street who helps us with 1). We take care of 2) ourselves, as the front door only opens very noisily.

The police help us with 3), called by the completely overwhelmed receptionist. Five policemen turn up. One of them speaks good English and translates for us. Once the situation is clear to everyone, we're allowed to stay in the hotel for one night.

In our room we celebrate our triumph over Chinese stubbornness in the hotel trade and drink to the kind policemen who really helped us out today. Our speedometer shows 127 kilometres and we're only a stone's throw from Kashgar... exhausted and very relieved, I fall asleep.

After a short night, the rest of 29 September starts with a lavish lunch. That makes the 130 kilometres to Shache bearable.

My first desert ride is sobering. Visibility is limited to a few kilometres by swirling sand. You can see neither the Himalayan mountains nor the next town. On top of that it's hot, muggy and windy. And that for weeks.

## Highlights of the desert days

1. **Listening to my iPod.** On shuffle, it plays me songs that fit the situation perfectly and make eternity feel a little shorter.
   Examples: _Peter Licht - Gerader Weg_ or _Keep On Moving Isolee Rmx_… Marvellous!
2. **Arriving at an oasis.** After the long sandy-brown phase, every speck of green is balm for the soul!
3. **Drinking half-frozen iced tea.** At first there are still regular stalls at the roadside selling cold drinks. Some of the bottles are frozen, which does a dry throat a world of good.
4. **A toilet with a working drain.** Long-drops are better than European toilets that don't flush.

## Low points

1. The bridge works along the road are very draining. Because of them we have to leave the good surface and carry on along dusty tracks. Most of these stretches are more than 40 kilometres long, and it takes hours before the road is passable again. My wrists also ache unpleasantly from pushing the bike through ankle-deep dusty sand so often. Ruts in the dust lead to minor falls (and tumbles).

2. Wrong information on our maps. I'm carrying three maps: a Chinese one (2009 edition), a German one (2008) and a Hungarian one (2007?). All three show different routes and towns. The Chinese map even marks every toilet hut as a town (white dot), which can have fatal consequences, especially in the even more barren, waterless desert of Qinghai!

3. Wind. From 11 am to 7 pm such a stiff breeze blows along the road that, despite putting your whole body into it, your speed is well below average. The only remedy for this torment: riding at night.

And so the days in the Taklamakan Desert go by, and on 16 October we reach Qinghai via one of the longest and most exhausting climbs. After the desert my muscles are empty. There are no easily digestible proteins to build up the muscles, and the flour-based food is too sweet to provide lasting energy.

In Qinghai the Qaidam Basin awaits us with a grey sandy desert and - NEW! - interesting rocks. There's even a lake in sight! Riding here is a lot more fun!

The first sizeable town, Huatugou, with its many restaurants and internet cafés, invites us to take a rest day. I spend most of the time eating and uploading pictures to Flickr.

On 18 September it's back into the desert. Today I've been on the road for 5 months, but I'm not in the mood to celebrate. At temperatures of around -10 degrees Celsius it's out of the sleeping bag in the morning. During the day the thermometer reaches 10 degrees at most, and at some point even the most beautiful rocky landscape is just... rocky landscape.

The highlights of these days are my broken rim, which I only notice 100 kilometres after Huatugou, and the bus ride to Delingha (see below).

If there were more places to stock up in this desert, it would be far more interesting. I liked the scenery here better than in the Taklamakan Desert.

Still, I'm glad the landscape is filling up with green again.

I'm riding through deep green gorges towards Xining and I'm curious to see how the landscape changes around Chengdu - my next destination.
