## So How Do You Come Up With an Idea Like That?

The idea for my bike trip was born at around 5 in the morning, after a rather colourful night out in my home town of Dieburg, Germany.

The sun is coming up, the birds are singing, my head is pleasantly fuzzy.

I go online to chat with my friend in China (Guangzhou) and assure him, once again and with great conviction, that I'm definitely coming to visit him. He'd just have to wait about a year. Because I'm coming by bike.

Before that, I'd been alone with my idea for months. I'd also done a first [test ride to Budapest]({{< ref "docs/2007/09" >}}), to see whether I really enjoy cycle touring.

My first thoughts of a longer bike tour sparked one day as I was heading home again on my favourite route.

Everything's running smoothly, I could keep going for hours. Completely in tune with nature. That most beautiful of all beautiful moments in a cyclist's life.

What's behind the next hill? How does the landscape change? Who will I meet? What adventures are waiting for me?

A first wish takes shape and I start digging up more cyclists' stories, reading travel reports, collecting checklists. Along the way I get to know some very interesting people who've done it before. I'm in good company.

I devour the books, I feel very close to them. I can relate to their stories so well, feel them so directly. Be part of it. I sense that I want this too. More and more. Feel it even more directly. Me. Experience it.

The first hurdle is accepting this idea myself - this crazy idea, based on nothing more than a wish and a few dreamy images. That may well be the biggest hurdle of all.

Saying out loud for the first time what had only been a tentative wish. Letting the jumble in my head become reality.

The more I talk the idea through with friends and family - or try to - the more determined I become to really do it.

Many are instantly enthusiastic, but after the first sentence the first thoughts about dangers and risks usually creep in, until the whole conversation is only about risks and dangers.

There's hardly a conversation in which I don't have to practise my patience and point out, again and again, that I'm aware of all the risks (see [risk list]({{< ref "docs/preparation/risk-management/#risk-list" >}})).

Soon after, I stop talking about my plans altogether. I'd rather turn inwards, concentrate on the planning and focus all my attention on myself.

The experiences of other travellers motivate me to keep going. I paint situations vividly in my mind, turn them into little films and imagine seeing the Himalayas, rolling into China, feeling the desert and arriving at Daniel's in Guangzhou and Ralph's in Singapore. I haven't the faintest idea about China, let alone Singapore or the countries in between.

So far my experience is based entirely on internet knowledge and other people's stories.

### You're really something special

Really not. As a child I hardly ever left Germany. I was perfectly happy as things were. No interest in other countries. The annual summer holiday in Italy - usually Tuscany - was enough for me.

Then, as a teenager, a trip to the Caribbean. That ride is a blur, lost in the haze of youth. All I remember are my first diving courses. Flying underwater, floating lightly, being free. A little escape from the hard everyday life that mostly revolved around family problems I had no answers to.

A few years later I explore Egypt on the usual tourist trails and say "hello!" to dolphins in the Red Sea, take a language course in Spain and discover Mallorca for road cycling.

More and more I realise that I really love cycling. Especially long, quiet rides suit me. At this point, though, the idea of ever doing longer tours and exploring the world that way simply isn't on my radar.

Suddenly it's there. So clear in front of me. I just have to do it. Above all: allow it. Open myself up to it.

Markus and Stefan T. rave to me about Thailand and warm nights in the pool. That sounds magical. I want to experience that too.

I think it's Steen / Steven (?), who rides his mountain bike through Scotland, whom I quiz enthusiastically at the medieval market in Dieburg. His quiet, reserved manner means I barely get any information out of him, so I end up working through the questions and researching them myself.

And planning. I read every book there is about cycle touring, visit the tourism fair in Frankfurt and meet Tilmann Waldthaler, whom I'd only known from his books until then. There I see the bikes that went travelling with him.

More and more I realise that travelling isn't rocket science; it's more about letting it happen, daring, _doing_, actually 'putting it into practice'. No waffle, just doing.

That attitude becomes my new motto.

In this context I vividly remember the [Selbstreise Handbuch](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Zentrale_f%C3%BCr_Globetrotter#Handbuch_der_Globetrotter) (a German handbook on independent travel) from [Peter Meyer Verlag](https://www.petermeyerverlag.de/).

See also the Deutsche Zentrale für Globetrotter (German centre for globetrotters) at <https://globetrotter.org/>

It shows me in a very practical way that the journey is, above all, about me. The book gives me lots of new insights and takes away many of my worries. Every day I see myself on the bike more clearly, and I'm really looking forward to giving it a go. Or rather: to finding out whether travelling really is that easy.

### How this book came about

I'm writing these lines more than six years later. I find it hard to transcribe my diary notes, and I've very successfully put it off for several years.

Today I can hardly believe that I actually went on this journey.

I've only edited the original texts minimally, so a fair bit of (non)sense has survived.

Looking back, many of my notes and comments seem rather naive. So in some passages I've added my current view and insights as a comment.

Anyway. This book comes from a certain distance to the journey.

Times always refer to local time in the respective country.

### Thank you, Alf

Alf (Daubner)'s checklist helped me enormously, and I recommend that anyone planning a longer trip makes a checklist and learns from others!

- [Alf's travel checklist](http://www.jordan-peter.de/equip/alfs_reisecheckliste.pdf) (in German)

On top of the checklist, I made detailed fact sheets with checklists for each country: map, position on the world map and the key facts about politics and entry requirements, based on the recommendations, advice and visa rules of the German Foreign Office.

This turns into a kind of reference work for my trip - meant more for those staying at home. And for me, to get a first impression.

I'd also put together a document with the most important questions and answers:

- Who am I?
- Do I have a family?
- Am I married?
- Where do I live? Where are you from?
- What is Germany known for?
- Do you have pictures from home? What's it like where you live?
- What do I do?
- What did I train as?
- What are my hobbies?
- Why am I travelling by bike?
- How long have you been on the road?
- Why did you pick this particular country?
- Where am I heading? What goal do I want to reach?
- Why don't I just fly straight to China?
- Why don't I go by car?
- Aren't you scared?
- Do you miss home? Your wife? Your family?
- Are you on the run?
- What makes you special?
- Who should be contacted in an emergency?
- Which countries have you already travelled to / which ones do you still want to visit?
- How do you finance your trip?
- How do we stay in touch?
- Why do I have syringes in my luggage?
- Why do I have pepper spray in my luggage?
- Can you take me with you to Germany?

I even had these answers translated into Russian. Thank you, Diana!

I think this list nicely reflects how I saw the world back then, which is why it mostly amuses me today. At best it's useful for a bit of self-reflection.

During my trip it was _not_ useful, because nobody ever wanted to know such details about me (more like 'where from - where to'). And anyone can answer these questions without lugging the paper around.

### Insurance

This mattered to me. I took out travel health insurance for one year.

Remember: ask for the insurance certificate in English!

### Schedule

Very important.

My knowledge of classic project planning and Gantt charts helped me a lot in gathering the best climate and visa conditions and comparing them clearly.

That way I get a (first) impression of what I'm planning, and I can see when things clash, for example arriving in the Himalayas in winter.

In hindsight, it was all very impressive and thought out on a very grand scale.

For example, I wanted to go to India… all in one year… Looking back, very funny.

The schedule is a very useful document, even if the plans get turned upside down all the time during the trip.
